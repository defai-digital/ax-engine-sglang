# NCM as a lossless speculative drafter

Scope: whether Next-Concept Modeling (Kumbong et al., arXiv:2610.14162v1, 9 Oct 2026, Hazy Research / Stanford) or any discrete/continuous concept hierarchy has been used as a **draft** model verified by an autoregressive LLM (lossless speculative decoding), and whether the paper’s own generation procedure and metrics would transfer to that use. Primary source is the v1 PDF/HTML. Secondary posts are labeled as such. Numbers below are copied from the paper text or from PDF text-layer labels; figure bar heights that are only drawn, not printed, are marked as not extracted.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14162 (HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1, PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162). Code: https://github.com/HazyResearch/ncm.

## 1. How NCM samples, whether decoding is lossless w.r.t. a token AR model, and how quality is measured

### Takeaway
NCM is the generator. It samples a hierarchy of discrete codes coarse-to-fine and decodes the final cumulative reconstruction to tokens by argmax. That is not lossless speculative decoding against an autoregressive token model: there is no draft/verify/accept step and no claim that the output distribution matches an AR LM. Quality is generative perplexity plus unigram entropy, BLEU, ROUGE, and Sudoku board accuracy, plus NFE and H100 wall-clock — not MAUVE, bits-per-byte, or an FID-like score. They discuss code-level teacher forcing versus free-running generation and error accumulation, but they do not report a paired teacher-forced vs free-running **token** accuracy for the generator.

### Cited Findings
- Two-stage model: a multiscale residual VQ-VAE encodes text into K levels of discrete codes; a transformer then generates one level at a time, predicting all codes in a level in parallel. The NCM model (plus frozen tokenizer decoder) is the text generator. — [arXiv:2610.14162 abstract and §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1)
- Factorization: \(p_\theta(r_{1:K})=\prod_k p_\theta(r_k\mid\hat z^{(k-1)})\), and within a level codes are conditionally independent, \(p_\theta(r_k\mid\hat z^{(k-1)})=\prod_i p_\theta(r_k^i\mid\hat z^{(k-1)})\). After the last level, the tokenizer decoder maps \(\hat z^{(K)}\) to token distributions at all L positions. — [§3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1)
- Algorithm 2 (generation): start from \(\hat z^{(0)}=0\); for each level, one forward pass, “Sample all \(\ell_k\) codes” in parallel, add that level’s reconstruction; then “Decode \(\hat z^{(K)}\) to token distributions” and “Select output tokens by argmax.” — [§3.4, Algorithm 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Training predicts all K levels in one forward pass (teacher-forced codes, with optional corruption of conditioning codes). Targets stay the clean tokenizer codes. — [§3.4, Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Explicit train/inference gap, not a token-accuracy table: “During training, NCM uses teacher forcing and conditions on clean input codes; during inference, it conditions on its own generated codes. This difference creates opportunities for errors to accumulate across levels. Since codes within each level are generated in parallel, multiple errors can enter the reconstruction used to generate the next level.” Mitigation is random replacement of conditioning codes (uniform from the same level’s codebook), not AR verification. “Corruption based on the model’s actual errors may better match inference conditions; we leave this to future work.” — [Appendix F.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Tokenizer exact-token reconstruction (encode then decode the full hierarchy, not free-running generation), Table 1: OWT 99.99%, LM1B 99.86%, TinyStories 99.98%, XSum 99.79%, WMT14 98.79%, Sudoku easy/medium/hard 100.00%. Mean codebook utilization: 83.10, 89.31, 90.12, 86.20, 61.89, 80.73, 83.85, 80.73 percent. Prose: Sudoku 100%; other tasks “close to 100%”; utilization “from 62% to 90%.” One wrong token “can alter meaning, prevent code from running, or invalidate a Sudoku solution.” — [Table 1, §4.1, §2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- OWT tokenizer case study: 99.99% exact reconstruction “corresponding to approximately 0.01% incorrectly reconstructed tokens.” Error types include named entities, diacritics, numerals, usernames, markup, and word boundaries. Cumulative reconstruction accuracy rises across levels and reaches 99.99% at full resolution. — [Appendix G, Table 11, Figure 16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Unconditional metric: generative perplexity (Gen. PPL) under GPT-2-large, plus mean per-sample unigram entropy H, because lower Gen. PPL can come from less diversity. Compared at diversity close to the data. Reference models other than GPT-2-large are in Appendix E.1; MAUVE is not used. — [§4.2, Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1)
- Table 8 (GPT-2-large Gen. PPL / H; lower PPL better). Data: TinyStories 9.67 / 3.96; LM1B 53.51 / 4.31; OpenWebText 14.15 / 5.44. NCM: TinyStories 15.41 / 3.98 at 12 NFE; LM1B 93.05 / 4.31 at 12 NFE; OWT 83.12 / 5.42 at 32 NFE. Strong diffusion points: TinyStories FMLM+ 15.43 / 3.90 at 128 NFE and DUO 19.63 / 4.01 at 128; LM1B DUO 97.30 / 4.30 at 128, MDLM 115.45 / 4.33 at 128; OWT DUO 77.69 / 5.55 at 1024 NFE, FMLM+ 66.60 / 5.21 at 128 NFE (entropy below data). AR sample/greedy are reported on TinyStories (8.89 / 4.01 and 5.34 / 4.06 at 128 NFE) and OWT (35.45 / 5.58 and 1.07 / 1.65 at 1024 NFE); LM1B AR cells are blank (“–”). — [Table 8, Appendix E.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Headline speed, source-measured on one H100 SXM 80GB, 1,024 sequences, batch 16: LM1B, 11× fewer NFE than DUO and 21× faster wall-clock at better Gen. PPL; XSum, 16× fewer NFE than AURORA-LM and 31× faster “at comparable ROUGE.” Abstract: better Gen. PPL than strongest DLM on LM1B at 21×; “competitive” on OpenWebText at 10×; matches strongest DLM ROUGE-L on XSum at 31×. Conclusion: generation “10–21× faster on LM1B and OpenWebText”; XSum “31× faster.” — [Abstract, §4.6, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1)
- Conditional Table 2. WMT14 De–En BLEU: AR 25.2 (99M, 64 NFE); E2D2 24.8 (99M, 64); DUO 21.3 (205M, 64); MDLM 18.4 (99M, 64); NCM 22.6 (120M, 11); NCM+CFG 23.9 (22 NFE); NCM+MBR 25.2 (132 NFE). XSum ROUGE-1/2/L: AR 30.5/10.2/24.4 (64 NFE); AURORA-LM 34.7/12.2/26.6 (130M, 128 NFE); NCM 35.0/12.8/28.0 (88M, 8 NFE); AURORA-LM guided 36.3/13.4/28.6 (184 NFE); NCM+CFG 35.3/13.0/28.2 (16 NFE); NCM+MBR 37.3/14.0/29.6 (128 NFE). Unguided prose: XSum better than AURORA-LM at 16× fewer NFE; on WMT14 NCM trails only E2D2 among non-AR baselines at 5.8× fewer NFE. CFG counts as two NFEs. — [Table 2, §4.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Test-time selection is MBR among NCM samples, not acceptance against an AR model. “about 7% higher ROUGE-L than the strongest summarization baseline within the same sampling-time budget.” MBR sweep: +4.48 ROUGE-1 from K=1 to K=256 candidates; main table uses K=16 on XSum (128 NFE, 29.6 ROUGE-L) and K=12 on WMT14 (132 NFE, 25.2 BLEU). CFG peaks at guidance weight w=0.5 for all three XSum ROUGE metrics and w=1.0 for BLEU. — [§4 intro, Appendix F.4, Figures 14–15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Sudoku, completely solved boards (%), Table 3. NCM 25M, 12 NFE: easy 99.5, medium 96.5, hard 86.1. FMLM+ 29M, 16 NFE: 97.7 / 92.8 / 81.3. AR greedy 25M, 128 NFE: 14.6 / 5.1 / 1.0. AR sample: 13.9 / 5.1 / 0.6. Clue counts 40/35/30 for easy/medium/hard. — [Table 3, §4.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Training tokens (full-length positions, including tokenizer): LM1B 13.36B vs baselines’ 66B, “approximately 4.9× fewer,” with Gen. PPL 93.1 above the NCM bar vs DUO 97.3, MDLM 115.5, FLM 112.3, CANDI 124.6, RDLM 295.4. OpenWebText about 127.22B positions, “approximately 4× fewer” than the “500B-plus” of MDLM and DUO. Abstract/conclusion also say about 4–5× fewer training tokens. — [§4.6, Figure 7, Appendix C.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Architecture actually used (Table 5), not the 248k-vocab / d=512 / 2+2-layer setup in the assignment: text vocabularies 50,304 (OWT, GPT-2), 30,528 (LM1B), 8,192 (TinyStories), 50,304 (XSum), 151,680 (WMT14, Qwen3-0.6B), 12 (Sudoku). Levels K = 32 / 12 / 12 / 8 / 11 / 12. Codebook size per level 4,096 except LM1B 8,192 and Sudoku 16. Latent width d = 384 except Sudoku 256. Encoder blocks 0; decoder blocks 1 except LM1B 4. Target lengths 1024 / 128 / 128 / 48 / 64 / 89. — [Table 4–5, Appendix C.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Not found in the v1 paper text: the strings “lossless,” “acceptance,” “MAUVE,” “bits-per-byte” / “bits per byte,” “free-running,” and “teacher-forced” as an accuracy metric. “Teacher forcing” appears as the training-time code-conditioning description in Appendix F.3 only. No FID-like metric.

### Inferences
- Published NCM quality does not measure agreement with an AR token distribution, so it does not transfer as evidence that an NCM draft would have a high speculative-decoding acceptance rate. Gen. PPL is “how likely is this sample under GPT-2-large,” after argmax token decode; a lossless drafter is judged by how often a target AR model accepts the proposed tokens.
- Because the final token step is argmax of the VQ decoder, even a perfect code generator cannot emit the AR model’s distribution unless that decoder’s mode happens to match it. Tokenizer reconstruction error (0.01% on OWT up to 1.21% on WMT14, i.e. 100−98.79) is an additional irreducible mismatch for any scheme that must reproduce a specific token string.
- On the paper’s own Table 8, “better than the strongest DLM” is true for LM1B versus DUO at matched entropy, but on OpenWebText NCM’s 83.12 Gen. PPL is worse than DUO’s 77.69 and FMLM+’s 66.60; the abstract’s word is “competitive,” and the authors flag low-entropy low-PPL as partly reduced diversity. AR sample/greedy on TinyStories and OWT are often lower Gen. PPL than NCM; the speed claims are against diffusion models, not against those AR rows.

### Gaps
- Sampling temperature, top-k, or nucleus settings for “Sample all \(\ell_k\) codes” are not stated in Algorithm 2. Not found in the extracted text.
- No numeric teacher-forced code accuracy vs free-running code accuracy, and no free-running token accuracy, are reported. Only the qualitative mismatch and the corruption ablation (plot, not a printed Gen. PPL table) are given.
- Per-level cumulative reconstruction curves are plotted (Figure 16) but intermediate percentages other than the final 99.99% on OWT were not printed as text.

## 2. Does the paper mention speculative decoding, drafting, verification, acceptance, or lossless decoding?

### Takeaway
Speculative decoding appears once, as related work about a different system (DFlash), not as a method or future-work item for NCM. “Lossless” and “acceptance” do not occur. The only “verification” is minimum-Bayes-risk selection among NCM’s own samples. Limitations and future work do not propose using NCM as an AR drafter.

### Cited Findings
- Only speculative-decoding sentence in the paper: “diffusion drafters like DFlash [9] accelerate AR models through speculative decoding.” Citation: J. Chen, Y. Liang, and Z. Liu (2026) “DFlash: block diffusion for flash speculative decoding,” arXiv:2602.06036. Cited from the introduction only. — [§1 and reference 9](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1)
- The only “draft” token is inside that same phrase (“diffusion drafters like DFlash”). — [§1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1)
- “Verify” / “verification” hits: Figure 15 caption says extra compute can be used for “sampling and verification through MBR selection.” That is similarity among NCM candidates, not token-level accept/reject against an AR target. — [Appendix F.4, Figure 15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- “Acceptance” and “lossless”: not found in v1 PDF text.
- Future work that is actually written: fixed K and \(\ell_k\) even for short continuations; an input-dependent schedule; separate VQ-VAE vs end-to-end training; scaling past the 725M LM1B generators; “occasional artifacts at coarse-level transitions.” Separately, error-based (rather than uniform) code corruption is “left to future work.” Speculative decoding is not in that list. — [Appendix I; Appendix F.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)

### Inferences
- A reader who treats “verification” in the MBR caption as speculative decoding would be misreading the paper. The authors use the word for candidate selection inside NCM’s own sampler.

### Gaps
- No author footnote or appendix says “we tried NCM as a speculative drafter and it failed/succeeded.” Absence is from full-text search of v1, not from a statement “we did not try this.”

## 3. Has anyone plugged NCM codes or a VQ hierarchy into EAGLE, Medusa, or draft-and-verify?

### Takeaway
No paper, repo result, or measured acceptance rate was found that uses NCM codes (or this VQ hierarchy) as a draft verified by an autoregressive LLM. What exists is commentary and one co-author interview describing speculative decoding as a possible integration, not as an experiment.

### Cited Findings
- Searches for NCM together with speculative decoding, draft model, acceptance rate, EAGLE, and Medusa returned the NCM paper’s DFlash citation, the GitHub README, and secondary writeups. None reported an NCM–EAGLE, NCM–Medusa, or NCM draft-and-verify system with acceptance rates. — queries run 2026-11-14 against the public web; primary negative is the paper itself ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1)) and the repo README ([HazyResearch/ncm](https://github.com/HazyResearch/ncm))
- Commentator contrast, not an experiment: “speculative methods still verify against an autoregressive target and preserve that distribution, while NCM changes the generation schedule itself.” — [AI Lookout, 14 Oct 2026](https://ailookout.news/blog/stanford-hazy-ncm-next-concept-modeling-parallel-text-generation/)
- Third-party opinion, not a result: “The likeliest near term path is not NCM alone but NCM inside a larger system: a fast coarse to fine drafter whose output an autoregressive model verifies or polishes, similar to speculative decoding.” The same post says the author ran official NCM checkpoints against an AR model trained on the same data; that comparison is quality/speed, and the speculative hybrid is listed as an untested idea (“Hybrids”). — [DEV Community, 10 Nov 2026](https://dev.to/nishaanth_11/next-concept-modeling-i-ran-stanfords-new-text-generator-against-a-classic-llm-here-is-what-255l)
- Co-author interview, not in the paper: “Kumbong sees opportunities to integrate it into existing systems, including speculative decoding, where a faster model proposes tokens for a larger model to verify.” Same interview, also not in the paper: “NCM is 6x to 10x faster than strong autoregressive baselines and the speedup increases as we scale the sequence length,” and “We are currently working on a V2 expected to solve many of these tokenization problems.” — [TechTalks, 6 Nov 2026](https://bdtechtalks.com/2026/11/06/next-concept-modeling/)
- DFlash is a block-diffusion drafter for lossless AR acceleration, cited by NCM as prior art, not as an NCM result. A later secondary blurb describes DFlash as “over 6× lossless acceleration” vs EAGLE-3; that number is about DFlash, not NCM. — [NCM §1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14162v1); [RITS news page, 9 Nov 2026](https://rits.shanghai.nyu.edu/ai/next-concept-modeling-beats-diffusion-lms-at-10-31x-speed/)

### Inferences
- As of this search, “NCM as a lossless drafter” is an idea in interviews and blogs. It is not a measured result, so paper Gen. PPL / BLEU / Sudoku numbers cannot be read as acceptance rates.

### Gaps
- No GitHub issue, Hugging Face discussion, or follow-up preprint was found that implements the interview suggestion. The search was web-index based, not an exhaustive code search of every fork.
- The TechTalks “6x to 10x vs strong autoregressive baselines” quote is not backed by a table in arXiv v1. The paper’s wall-clock tables compare diffusion/flow models. Treat the 6–10× AR claim as interview-only until a methods note appears.
- OpenReview ICLR 2027 submission “Hierarchical Text Generation with Next-Concept Modeling (NCM)” uses different speed wording than arXiv v1 (“14× faster” on OpenWebText in the OpenReview abstract vs “10×” in arXiv v1; both say 21× and 93.05 vs 97.30 on LM1B). That page was not fully fetched; do not merge it with v1 numbers. — [OpenReview d83mBTrpiJ](https://openreview.net/forum?id=d83mBTrpiJ)

## 4. Ablations: what hurts, and what that says for a tokenizer at 66–84% reconstruction

### Takeaway
The paper never studies a tokenizer stuck near 66–84% exact reconstruction. Its TinyStories level-count ablation still reconstructs at least 99.95% for K in {4, 8, 16, 32}. Among printed generation-quality deltas, the level **schedule** moves Gen. PPL the most (about 57.7 vs 196.7). Loss-weight \(\alpha\) barely moves Gen. PPL (18.9–19.4). There is no codebook-size ablation. Corruption is described as mattering a lot, but the Gen. PPL values are in a figure, not a table. None of these ablations are acceptance rates under an AR verifier.

### Cited Findings
- Ablation list: latent hierarchy (number of levels, schedule), transformer (attention, context encoder, loss weighting), code corruption, sampling. Default ablation dataset is TinyStories unless noted. “Each experiment changes one design choice.” — [Appendix F](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Number of levels, K in {4, 8, 16, 32}, linear schedule from 1 to 128 codes, codebook 4,096 per level, tokenizer 40k steps, generator 10k steps, **no code corruption**. “Gen. PPL decreases as the number of levels grows… diminishing returns.” “All four reconstruct at least 99.95% of tokens.” “These ablations do not use code corruption, so errors can accumulate across levels; adding corruption may narrow the gaps between hierarchies.” Figure 9 text-layer labels: entropies H=4.22, 4.15, 4.11, 4.08 for K=4, 8, 16, 32, and relative annotations −52%, −32%, −20% on the final-checkpoint panel. Absolute Gen. PPL for those four bars is not printed (axis spans roughly 100–300). Reference text Gen. PPL 9.67, H=3.96. — [Appendix F.1.1, Figure 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Level schedule, K=12, 1 code to 128, final Gen. PPL at 10k steps (Figure 10b labels): square-root 196.7 (H=4.21), logarithmic 168.2 (H=4.20), linear 88.4 (H=4.09), cosine 77.8 (H=4.08), quadratic 59.6 (H=4.06), exponential 57.7 (H=4.05). — [Appendix F.1.2, Figure 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Attention, same 12-level tokenizer and mean corruption rate 0.50: block-causal Gen. PPL 19.63 (H=4.01, 7.90 s); two-level sliding window 17.84 (H=3.99, 6.83 s); block-diagonal 15.41 (H=3.98, 5.65 s). Times are 1,024 TinyStories samples, 12 steps, batch 16. Block-causal and sliding window are 1.40× and 1.21× the block-diagonal time. — [Table 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Loss weighting \(w_k \propto \ell_k^{-\alpha}\): \(\alpha=0\) equal weight per code (the ELBO case); \(\alpha=1\) equal weight per level. Raising \(\alpha\) from 0 to 1.5 moves the coarse levels’ share of the loss from 19% to 63% and lowers their validation loss, “yet Gen. PPL after 10k steps stays at 18.9–19.0 for \(\alpha\le 1\) and rises slightly to 19.4 at 1.5.” Figure 12 labels those six Gen. PPL values as 19.0, 18.9, 18.9, 19.0, 18.9, 19.4. — [Appendix F.2.3, Figure 12, Eq. 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Code corruption: uniform replacements, linearly decreasing rate across levels. “Moderate corruption greatly improves generation quality, while excessive corruption weakens the conditioning signal and degrades quality.” Figure 13 x positions include 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 plus a marked “recipe”; printed entropies include H=4.06, 4.00, 3.97, 3.97, 3.95, 3.96. Y-axis is about 20–50 Gen. PPL. Bar heights are not printed as numbers. The attention ablation calls the selected recipe “mean rate 0.50.” — [Appendix F.3, Figure 13; Table 9 setup](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Context encoder (XSum, not a hierarchy ablation): frozen T5-small vs the VQ-VAE encoder, greedy, 8 NFE. ROUGE-1/2/L 34.89/12.92/28.19 vs 34.79/12.77/27.95 (deltas +0.10 / +0.15 / +0.24) at +35.3M parameters (123.4M vs 88.0M). They keep the VQ-VAE encoder. — [Table 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)
- Codebook size is a hyperparameter in Table 5 (4,096, or 8,192 on LM1B, or 16 on Sudoku) but is not one of the ablations. No “smaller codebook” delta is reported.
- Main generators are ~12 blocks, hidden 768, d_latent 384 (Sudoku: 8 blocks, hidden 512, d=256). Not 2+2 layers and not d=512. — [Tables 5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14162)

### Inferences
- If the goal is ranking fixes for a **tokenizer** at 66–84% reconstruction, the paper does not supply that ranking. Every hierarchy they ablate already reconstructs ≥99.95% on TinyStories. Moving from K=4 to K=32 is described as only a slight reconstruction gain in that high-accuracy regime, while it still changes generation PPL a lot when corruption is off. A tokenizer at 66–84% is outside the measured range; more levels are not shown to close a ~15–35 point reconstruction gap.
- For **generation** quality at fixed tokenizer quality, the printed ranking of harm is: a bad growth schedule (square-root/log vs exponential, ~197 vs ~58 Gen. PPL) is larger than attention pattern (~19.6 vs 15.4) which is larger than loss weighting (~19.4 vs 18.9). Corruption is described as first-order (“greatly improves” then hurts if too high) but without printed PPL deltas, so it cannot be numerically ranked against the schedule from the text alone.
- None of these deltas are “what would raise AR acceptance.” Coarse-code errors are exactly the failure mode they train against, because one wrong coarse code changes the conditioning for every finer level. A 66–84% reconstruction tokenizer would feed that failure mode even before generator error. That is an inference from F.3 plus Table 1, not a measured acceptance rate.
- The user’s candidate ladders (block 32 with levels 4/8/16/32 or 1/2/4/8/16/32, 8192 codes, d=512, 2+2 layers, 248k vocab) do not match any reported NCM configuration. The closest paper settings are the K∈{4,8,16,32} TinyStories ablation (4096 codes, linear 1…128, high reconstruction) and LM1B’s 8192-code, K=12, d=384 tokenizer at 99.86% reconstruction.

### Gaps
- Numeric Gen. PPL for each corruption rate and for each K in Figure 9 are not in the text layer, only axes, entropies, and the three percentage annotations.
- No ablation of codebook size, latent width, depth (2+2 vs 12 blocks), or vocabulary size near 248k. WMT14 uses a 151,680 vocab and is the worst non-Sudoku reconstruction in Table 1 (98.79%), but vocab size is confounded with task, K=11, and d=384.
- No experiment links reconstruction accuracy to downstream Gen. PPL or to token-level match against an AR teacher.

## 5. README vs paper

### Takeaway
The README does not publish reconstruction or Gen. PPL numbers. Its only numeric quality/speed claim that differs from the paper is the headline range **9–31×** faster. The paper’s abstract and conclusion use **10×, 21×, and 31×** (and “10–21×” plus “31×”), not 9×. The Hazy Research blog uses **10–31×**.

### Cited Findings
- README highlight: “NCM beats or matches strong diffusion baselines on different tasks & datasets while generating 9-31× faster.” No reconstruction accuracy, Gen. PPL, BLEU, ROUGE, or Sudoku percentage appears in the README body. — [README.md](https://github.com/HazyResearch/ncm/blob/main/README.md)
- README evaluation entry points print ROUGE-1/2/L and Gen. PPL for XSum; cased and lowercased sacreBLEU, chrF, and length ratio for WMT14; solved, cell, and valid-grid rates for Sudoku; and GPT-2-large Gen. PPL plus unigram entropy over 5 seeds of 256 samples for TinyStories, OpenWebText, and LM1B. Those extra metric **names** (chrF, length ratio, cell rate, valid-grid rate) are not the headline tables of the paper; the README does not give values for them. — [README “Evaluation”](https://github.com/HazyResearch/ncm/blob/main/README.md)
- Paper ranges, for contrast: abstract “21×” (LM1B), “10×” (OpenWebText), “31×” (XSum); conclusion “10–21× faster on LM1B and OpenWebText” and “31×” on XSum. - Hazy Research blog TL;DR uses “10–31× faster” and “4–5× fewer training tokens,” matching the paper’s range rather than the README’s 9× floor. — [Hazy Research blog, 12 Oct 2026](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2026-10-12-ncm)
